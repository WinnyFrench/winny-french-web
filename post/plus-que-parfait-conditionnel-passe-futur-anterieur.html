Plus-que-parfait, Conditionnel Passé, Futur Antérieur: Just Change the Auxiliary

Nov 13, 2026 · 7 min read

Quick answer: If you can build the passé composé, you already know these three tenses. The passé composé is auxiliary (avoir or être) in the present plus the past participle: j'ai mangé (I have eaten). To get the other three, you keep the participle exactly as it is and just change the tense of the auxiliary. Put the auxiliary in the imparfait and you get the plus-que-parfait (I had eaten). Put it in the future and you get the futur antérieur (I will have eaten). Put it in the conditionnel and you get the conditionnel passé (I would have eaten). That is the whole trick.

A quick note on the name: the third tense is the futur antérieur ("future anterior"), sometimes misremembered as "futur intérieur." It means "will have done."

The one idea to hold onto

French has a family of past tenses that are all built the same way: a helper verb (the auxiliaire) plus a past participle. The participle is the fixed part. It never changes across these four tenses. The only thing that moves is the tense of the little helper word in front of it. Learn to swap that one word and three new tenses fall out for free.

English works in the exact same way, which is why this is easier than it looks. English stacks its tenses on the helper word have: I have eaten, I had eaten, I will have eaten, I would have eaten. The word "eaten" never moves. Only "have" changes. French does the same thing with avoir.

See it on one verb: manger (avoir verb)

Watch only the highlighted word change. The participle mangé ("eaten") stays put the whole way down.

Tense French English What the auxiliary is doing
Passé composé j'ai mangé I have eaten / I ate avoir in the present
Plus-que-parfait j'avais mangé I had eaten avoir in the imparfait
Futur antérieur j'aurai mangé I will have eaten avoir in the futur simple
Conditionnel passé j'aurais mangé I would have eaten avoir in the conditionnel

Look at the two highlighted columns together. Every time the French auxiliary shifts (ai, avais, aurai, aurais), the English helper shifts in lockstep (have, had, will have, would have). Same move, two languages.

The same works for être verbs (Dr and Mrs Vandertramp and reflexives)

A handful of verbs build their compound tenses with être instead of avoir. These are the movement and change-of-state verbs often remembered as Dr and Mrs Vandertramp (devenir, revenir, monter, rester, sortir, venir, aller, naître, descendre, entrer, retourner, tomber, rentrer, arriver, mourir, partir, and a few more), plus all reflexive verbs (se laver, se lever, and so on). The rule does not change at all. You still keep the participle and just move the auxiliary into the tense you want. The only extra thing to remember is that with être the participle agrees with the subject (allé, allée, allés, allées), and that agreement is the same in all four tenses.

Tense French (aller, to go) English
Passé composé je suis allé(e) I have gone / I went
Plus-que-parfait j'étais allé(e) I had gone
Futur antérieur je serai allé(e) I will have gone
Conditionnel passé je serais allé(e) I would have gone

What each tense actually means

Plus-que-parfait: "had done" (the past before the past)

The plus-que-parfait is the auxiliary in the imparfait. It describes something that had already happened before another past moment. In English it is the "had done" tense.

FrenchEnglish
J'avais déjà mangé quand il est arrivé.I had already eaten when he arrived.
Elle était partie avant la fin.She had left before the end.

Futur antérieur: "will have done" (finished before a future point)

The futur antérieur is the auxiliary in the futur simple. It describes something that will be finished before some later moment in the future. In English it is the "will have done" tense.

FrenchEnglish
J'aurai fini avant midi.I will have finished before noon.
Quand tu arriveras, nous serons partis.When you arrive, we will have left.

Conditionnel passé: "would have done" (the past that never happened)

The conditionnel passé is the auxiliary in the conditionnel. It describes something that would have happened under a condition that was not met. It is the tense of hindsight and regret, and in English it is the "would have done" tense.

FrenchEnglish
J'aurais mangé si j'avais eu faim.I would have eaten if I had been hungry.
Nous serions venus plus tôt.We would have come earlier.

That last example is a classic pairing: si plus the plus-que-parfait in one half, and the conditionnel passé in the other. "If I had been hungry, I would have eaten." English lines up with French word for word.

Frequently asked questions

How are the plus-que-parfait, futur antérieur and conditionnel passé formed?

All three use the same two pieces as the passé composé: an auxiliary (avoir or être) and a past participle. You keep the participle and change only the tense of the auxiliary. Auxiliary in the imparfait gives the plus-que-parfait (j'avais mangé, I had eaten), in the futur simple gives the futur antérieur (j'aurai mangé, I will have eaten), and in the conditionnel gives the conditionnel passé (j'aurais mangé, I would have eaten).

What is the difference between the passé composé and the plus-que-parfait?

The passé composé has the auxiliary in the present and means "I did" or "I have done." The plus-que-parfait has the auxiliary in the imparfait and means "I had done." The plus-que-parfait describes an action that happened before another past action: "J'avais déjà mangé quand il est arrivé" means "I had already eaten when he arrived."

Is it futur antérieur or futur intérieur?

It is the futur antérieur. It is the auxiliary in the futur simple plus a past participle, and it means "will have done," as in "J'aurai fini avant midi" (I will have finished before noon).

Do être verbs follow the same rule?

Yes. Dr and Mrs Vandertramp verbs and all reflexive verbs build these tenses with être instead of avoir, but the rule is identical: keep the participle and change the tense of être. The only extra step is that the participle agrees with the subject (allé, allée, allés, allées), and that agreement stays the same in all four tenses.

Does the past participle change between these tenses?

No. The past participle is the fixed part. Whether you say j'ai mangé, j'avais mangé, j'aurai mangé or j'aurais mangé, the participle "mangé" never changes. Only the auxiliary moves.
