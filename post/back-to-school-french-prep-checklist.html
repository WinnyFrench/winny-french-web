Back-to-School French Checklist for a Strong Year (Grades 1 to 12)

Aug 30, 2026 · 5 min read

Short answer: The families who have the smoothest French year are not the ones who cram in September. They are the ones who set up a few small habits in the first two weeks, then catch problems early instead of at report-card time. Here is a grade-by-grade checklist to do exactly that, whether your child is in core French, extended French, or immersion.

The 15-minute setup for week one

Before the first quiz comes home, do these five things. They take about fifteen minutes total and save you a scramble later.

  • Find out which stream your child is in this year: core French, extended French, or immersion. The workload and the marking are very different.
  • Ask the teacher, by email is fine, what the first unit covers and how French is assessed this term.
  • Set a fixed ten-minute French slot in the daily routine. Same time every day beats a long session once a week.
  • Put the exam or evaluation dates you already know into a shared calendar, especially any DELF or high school French deadlines.
  • Decide now what "falling behind" will look like to you, so you notice it in week three, not in November.

Grades 1 to 3: build the ear first

In the early grades, and in Early French Immersion where French starts in Grade 1, the goal is comfort with the sounds and rhythm of the language, not perfect grammar. Read simple French picture books aloud together even if your own accent is rough. Let your child watch a favourite show dubbed in French. The point is exposure and confidence, so keep it light and never turn it into a test.

Grades 4 to 6: the workload steps up

Core French becomes mandatory in Grade 4 across Ontario, and immersion students start carrying real subject content in French. This is where small gaps first appear. Watch for vocabulary that is not sticking and verbs that are always guessed. Ten minutes a day of review, plus reading back the week's new words out loud, keeps these gaps from compounding.

Grades 7 and 8: writing becomes the challenge

By Grade 7 and 8, French marks depend less on memorized vocabulary and more on writing full, correct sentences. Verb tenses are the usual sticking point, especially the difference between the passé composé and the imparfait. If your child can speak but freezes when they have to write, that is the signal to add structured practice now, before high school raises the stakes.

Grades 9 to 12: protect the credit and the options

High school French is a credit that shows up on transcripts, and for many students it is the year they decide whether to keep French past the mandatory Grade 9 course. Keep the option open. Stay on top of oral presentations and written assignments, and if your teen is aiming for a DELF certificate or a bilingual designation, start that preparation early rather than in the final month.

How to tell early if your child needs help

You do not need to speak French to spot trouble. The reliable early signs are simple: homework in French takes far longer than other subjects, your child avoids talking about French class, marks slip on the first two evaluations, or they say some version of "I just do not get it." One of these is worth a conversation with the teacher. Two or more, and a few weeks with a tutor usually turns it around faster than waiting.

The one habit that beats cramming

If you do nothing else from this list, do this: ten minutes of French every day, out loud, is worth more than an hour of silent review on the weekend. Language lives in frequent, spoken practice. A short daily rhythm, a patient adult or tutor to talk to, and early attention when something slips is the whole formula for a strong French year.

Frequently asked questions

When should we start tutoring if my child is struggling?

Earlier is easier. A gap caught in September is a few weeks of work, while the same gap caught in January has usually spread into new units. If the first two evaluations come back low, that is the moment to act.

My child is in immersion and I do not speak French. Can I still help?

Yes. You do not need French to build the routine, keep an eye on the early warning signs, and talk to the teacher. For the actual French, a tutor who knows the immersion curriculum can do the part you cannot.
